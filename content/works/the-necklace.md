## 原文
The girl was one of those pretty and charming young creatures who sometimes are born, as if by a slip of fate, into a family of clerks. She had no dowry, no expectations, no way of being known, understood, loved, married by any rich and distinguished man; so she let herself be married to a little clerk of the Ministry of Public Instruction.

She dressed plainly because she could not dress well, but she was unhappy as if she had really fallen from a higher station; since with women there is neither caste nor rank, for beauty, grace and charm take the place of family and birth. Natural ingenuity, instinct for what is elegant, a supple mind are their sole hierarchy, and often make of women of the people the equals of the very greatest ladies.

Mathilde suffered ceaselessly, feeling herself born to enjoy all delicacies and all luxuries. She was distressed at the poverty of her dwelling, at the bareness of the walls, at the shabby chairs, the ugliness of the curtains. All those things, of which another woman of her rank would never even have been conscious, tortured her and made her angry. The sight of the little Breton peasant who did her humble housework aroused in her despairing regrets and bewildering dreams. She thought of silent antechambers hung with Oriental tapestry, illumined by tall bronze candelabra, and of two great footmen in knee breeches who sleep in the big armchairs, made drowsy by the oppressive heat of the stove. She thought of long reception halls hung with ancient silk, of the dainty cabinets containing priceless curiosities and of the little coquettish perfumed reception rooms made for chatting at five o'clock with intimate friends, with men famous and sought after, whom all women envy and whose attention they all desire.

When she sat down to dinner, before the round table covered with a tablecloth in use three days, opposite her husband, who uncovered the soup tureen and declared with a delighted air, “Ah, the good soup! I don't know anything better than that,” she thought of dainty dinners, of shining silverware, of tapestry that peopled the walls with ancient personages and with strange birds flying in the midst of a fairy forest; and she thought of delicious dishes served on marvellous plates and of the whispered gallantries to which you listen with a sphinxlike smile while you are eating the pink meat of a trout or the wings of a quail.

She had no gowns, no jewels, nothing. And she loved nothing but that. She felt made for that. She would have liked so much to please, to be envied, to be charming, to be sought after.

She had a friend, a former schoolmate at the convent, who was rich, and whom she did not like to go to see any more because she felt so sad when she came home.

But one evening her husband reached home with a triumphant air and holding a large envelope in his hand.

“There,” said he, “there is something for you.”

She tore the paper quickly and drew out a printed card which bore these words:

The Minister of Public Instruction and Madame Georges Ramponneau request the honor of M. and Madame Loisel's company at the palace of the Ministry on Monday evening, January 18th.

Instead of being delighted, as her husband had hoped, she threw the invitation on the table crossly, muttering:

“What do you wish me to do with that?”

“Why, my dear, I thought you would be glad. You never go out, and this is such a fine opportunity. I had great trouble to get it. Every one wants to go; it is very select, and they are not giving many invitations to clerks. The whole official world will be there.”

She looked at him with an irritated glance and said impatiently:

“And what do you wish me to put on my back?”

He had not thought of that. He stammered:

“Why, the gown you go to the theatre in. It looks very well to me.”

He stopped, distracted, seeing that his wife was weeping. Two great tears ran slowly from the corners of her eyes toward the corners of her mouth.

“What's the matter? What's the matter?” he answered.

By a violent effort she conquered her grief and replied in a calm voice, while she wiped her wet cheeks:

“Nothing. Only I have no gown, and, therefore, I can't go to this ball. Give your card to some colleague whose wife is better equipped than I am.”

## 白话
那个女孩是那种漂亮而迷人的年轻女子中的一员，有时似乎命运开了个玩笑，把她生在了职员家庭里。她没有嫁妆，没有期望，没有被认识、理解、爱慕、嫁给某个富有的、有声望的男人的可能；于是她听从命运，嫁给了一个教育部的小职员。

她穿着朴素，因为她穿不出华贵的衣服，但她仿佛真的从更高的地位跌落下来一样感到不快；因为对女人来说，没有种姓也没有等级，美貌、优雅和魅力取代了家族和出身。天生的聪慧、对优雅事物的本能、灵活的头脑，构成了她们唯一的等级制度，常常使出身平民的女子成为最伟大的贵妇人的平等者。

马蒂尔德痛苦不堪，觉得自己生来就是为了享受所有精致的美食和奢华的生活。她为自己的住所贫穷而苦恼，为墙壁的空旷、椅子的破旧、窗帘的丑陋而烦恼。这些对同阶层的女子来说根本不会察觉到的事物，却折磨着她，使她愤怒。看到那个做着粗重家务的小布列塔尼农妇，她心中涌起绝望的悔恨和迷离的幻想。她想象着那些挂满东方挂毯的静室，被高大的青铜烛台照亮，还有两个穿着膝上短裤的壮实仆人，懒洋洋地坐在大扶手椅里，被壁炉的闷热弄得昏昏欲睡。她想象着长长的接待厅，挂满古旧丝绸，精致的陈列柜里存放着无价的珍品，还有那些小巧玲珑、香气四溢的会客厅，人们在五点左右与亲密的朋友、那些著名而受追捧的男士们闲聊，所有女人都羡慕他们，也都渴望他们的关注。

当她坐在圆桌旁，桌布已经用了三天，对面是她的丈夫，他揭开汤盘，带着欣喜的神情说道：“啊，这汤真好！我从没吃过比这更好的。”她却想着那些精致的晚餐，闪亮的银器，挂满古代人物和奇异飞鸟的挂毯；她想着那些盛在奇妙盘子上的美味佳肴，还有那些低声细语的奉承之词，你带着斯芬克斯般的微笑，一边吃着鳟鱼的粉红肉，一边听着那些甜言蜜语，或是吃着鹌鹑的翅膀。

她没有连衣裙，没有珠宝，什么也没有。而她唯一热爱的就是这些。她觉得自己天生就是为此而生的。她多么希望能讨人喜欢，被人羡慕，被人迷恋，被人追求。

她有一个朋友，是以前在修道院的同学，家境富裕，但她不再想去见她，因为每次回家，她都感到十分沮丧。

但有一天晚上，她的丈夫带着一副胜利的神情回家，手里拿着一个大信封。

“喏，”他说，“这是给你的。”

她迅速撕开信纸，抽出一张印着字的请柬：

教育部部长及夫人乔治·拉普诺奈请求洛伊塞尔先生与夫人光临教育部宫殿，于1月18日（星期一）晚上莅临。

她并没有像丈夫所希望的那样高兴，而是不耐烦地把请柬扔在桌上，嘟囔着：

“你让我拿这个干什么？”

“亲爱的，我想你一定很高兴。你从不外出，这可是个很好的机会。我费了好大劲才弄到这张请柬。大家都想参加，这很特别，他们只给少数职员发邀请。整个官场的人都会到场。”

她用愤怒的眼神看着他，不耐烦地说：

“那你希望我穿什么去？”

他没想到这一点，结结巴巴地说：

“啊，你去剧院穿的那件裙子，我觉得挺合适的。”

他停了下来，心神不宁地看到妻子在流泪。两颗大大的泪珠慢慢地从她眼角流到嘴角。

“怎么了？怎么了？”他回答道。

她强忍着悲伤，用平静的声音回答，同时擦着湿润的脸颊：

“没什么。只是我没有连衣裙，所以不能去参加这个舞会。把你的请柬给某个妻子更有条件的同事吧。”
