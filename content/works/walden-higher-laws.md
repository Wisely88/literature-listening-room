## 原文
As I came home through the woods with my string of fish, trailing my pole, it being now quite dark, I caught a glimpse of a woodchuck stealing across my path, and felt a strange thrill of savage delight, and was strongly tempted to seize and devour him raw; not that I was hungry then, except for that wildness which he represented. Once or twice, however, while I lived at the pond, I found myself ranging the woods, like a half-starved hound, with a strange abandonment, seeking some kind of venison which I might devour, and no morsel could have been too savage for me. The wildest scenes had become unaccountably familiar. I found in myself, and still find, an instinct toward a higher, or, as it is named, spiritual life, as do most men, and another toward a primitive rank and savage one, and I reverence them both. I love the wild not less than the good. The wildness and adventure that are in fishing still recommended it to me. I like sometimes to take rank hold on life and spend my day more as the animals do. Perhaps I have owed to this employment and to hunting, when quite young, my closest acquaintance with Nature. They early introduce us to and detain us in scenery with which otherwise, at that age, we should have little acquaintance. Fishermen, hunters, woodchoppers, and others, spending their lives in the fields and woods, in a peculiar sense a part of Nature themselves, are often in a more favorable mood for observing her, in the intervals of their pursuits, than philosophers or poets even, who approach her with expectation. She is not afraid to exhibit herself to them. The traveller on the prairie is naturally a hunter, on the head waters of the Missouri and Columbia a trapper, and at the Falls of St. Mary a fisherman. He who is only a traveller learns things at second-hand and by the halves, and is poor authority. We are most interested when science reports what those men already know practically or instinctively, for that alone is a true humanity, or account of human experience.

They mistake who assert that the Yankee has few amusements, because he has not so many public holidays, and men and boys do not play so many games as they do in England, for here the more primitive but solitary amusements of hunting fishing and the like have not yet given place to the former. Almost every New England boy among my contemporaries shouldered a fowling piece between the ages of ten and fourteen; and his hunting and fishing grounds were not limited, like the preserves of an English nobleman, but were more boundless even than those of a savage. No wonder, then, that he did not oftener stay to play on the common. But already a change is taking place, owing, not to an increased humanity, but to an increased scarcity of game, for perhaps the hunter is the greatest friend of the animals hunted, not excepting the Humane Society.

Moreover, when at the pond, I wished sometimes to add fish to my fare for variety. I have actually fished from the same kind of necessity that the first fishers did. Whatever humanity I might conjure up against it was all factitious, and concerned my philosophy more than my feelings. I speak of fishing only now, for I had long felt differently about fowling, and sold my gun before I went to the woods. Not that I am less humane than others, but I did not perceive that my feelings were much affected. I did not pity the fishes nor the worms. This was habit. As for fowling, during the last years that I carried a gun my excuse was that I was studying ornithology, and sought only new or rare birds. But I confess that I am now inclined to think that there is a finer way of studying ornithology than this. It requires so much closer attention to the habits of the birds, that, if for that reason only, I have been willing to omit the gun. Yet notwithstanding the objection on the score of humanity, I am compelled to doubt if equally valuable sports are ever substituted for these; and when some of my friends have asked me anxiously about their boys, whether they should let them hunt, I have answered, yes,—remembering that it was one of the best parts of my education,—make them hunters, though sportsmen only at first, if possible, mighty hunters at last, so that they shall not find game large enough for them in this or any vegetable wilderness,—hunters as well as fishers of men.

## 白话
当我提着一串鱼，拖着钓竿，穿过树林回家时，天色已完全暗下来。我瞥见一只土拨鼠偷偷地横穿我的小路，顿时感到一种奇异的野性快感，内心强烈地渴望抓住它，生吃掉它；并不是因为那时我饿，而是因为那野性所代表的野性。然而，我住在池塘期间，曾有几次，我像一只半饿的猎犬一样，漫无目的地在树林中游荡，带着一种异样的执着，寻找某种野味来吃，任何野性十足的食物对我来说都不嫌太野。那些最狂野的场景，竟变得难以解释地熟悉。我发现自己，也依然发现，有一种本能趋向于一种更高、或者说，名为精神的生存方式，如同大多数人类一样；同时，我也有一种本能趋向于原始、粗野的生存方式，我同样敬重这两种。我热爱野性，不亚于热爱善。钓鱼中所蕴含的野性与冒险，仍然吸引着我。有时，我喜欢牢牢把握生活，把一天过得更像动物那样。也许正是由于这种职业和狩猎，我在年轻时才与自然有最亲密的接触。他们早早就将我们引入自然的景色中，并将我们留在那里，否则在那个年纪，我们本不会有多少机会接触到自然。渔夫、猎人、伐木工以及其他在田野和树林中度过一生的人，以某种特殊的方式，本身就是自然的一部分，他们在从事工作之余，往往比哲学家或诗人更易以一种良好的心态去观察自然。她并不害怕向他们展示自己。在草原上旅行的人，自然就是猎人；在密苏里河和哥伦比亚河源头的人，自然就是捕兽人；在圣玛丽瀑布的人，自然就是渔夫。只是一名旅行者，他所学到的总是二手的、不完整的知识，他也不足以成为权威。我们最感兴趣的是，当科学报告那些人已经实践或本能地知道的东西，因为那才是真正的“人性”，或对人类经验的真实记录。

那些认为新英格兰人娱乐方式不多的人是错误的，因为他们没有那么多公共假日，男人和男孩也不像在英国那样玩很多游戏。在这里，那些更原始但孤独的娱乐方式——狩猎、钓鱼等——尚未被取代。几乎每一个我同时代的新英格兰男孩，在十到十四岁之间都曾扛着猎枪；他们的狩猎和钓鱼范围并不像英国贵族的猎场那样有限，甚至比野人更广阔。难怪他们不常留在公共场合玩耍。但如今，一种变化正在发生，这并非由于人类变得更仁慈，而是由于猎物变得更稀少，也许猎人反而是那些被猎动物最好的朋友，包括人道协会在内。

此外，当我住在池塘边时，有时也想通过增加鱼来丰富我的饮食。我实际上是以同样的必要性去钓鱼，就像最早的渔夫那样。无论我如何唤起自己的人性来反对这种行为，那都是人为的，更多地影响了我的哲学，而非我的情感。我此刻只谈论钓鱼，因为我很久以前对打猎就持不同看法，并在我进入树林之前就卖掉了我的枪。并不是我比别人更不仁慈，只是我没有意识到我的情感受到了多大的影响。我并不怜悯那些鱼或虫子。这已成习惯。至于打猎，我最后一次持枪的那些年，我的借口是我在研究鸟类学，只寻找新奇或稀有的鸟类。但我承认，我现在倾向于认为，研究鸟类学还有更优的方式。它需要更密切地关注鸟类的习性，因此，仅凭这一点，我就愿意放弃猎枪。然而，尽管有人类道德方面的反对意见，我仍不得不怀疑，是否还有同样有价值的娱乐活动可以取代这些；当一些朋友焦急地问我，是否应该让他们的孩子去打猎时，我回答说，应该让他们去——我记得这曾是我教育中最好的部分——让他们成为猎人，即使一开始只是普通的猎人，如果可能的话，最终成为强大的猎人，这样他们就不会在这片或任何植物覆盖的荒野中找到足够的猎物——成为人类的猎人和渔夫。
