## 原文
Once it chanced that I stood in the very abutment of a rainbow’s arch, which filled the lower stratum of the atmosphere, tinging the grass and leaves around, and dazzling me as if I looked through colored crystal. It was a lake of rainbow light, in which, for a short while, I lived like a dolphin. If it had lasted longer it might have tinged my employments and life. As I walked on the railroad causeway, I used to wonder at the halo of light around my shadow, and would fain fancy myself one of the elect. One who visited me declared that the shadows of some Irishmen before him had no halo about them, that it was only natives that were so distinguished. Benvenuto Cellini tells us in his memoirs, that, after a certain terrible dream or vision which he had during his confinement in the castle of St. Angelo, a resplendent light appeared over the shadow of his head at morning and evening, whether he was in Italy or France, and it was particularly conspicuous when the grass was moist with dew. This was probably the same phenomenon to which I have referred, which is especially observed in the morning, but also at other times, and even by moonlight. Though a constant one, it is not commonly noticed, and, in the case of an excitable imagination like Cellini’s, it would be basis enough for superstition. Beside, he tells us that he showed it to very few. But are they not indeed distinguished who are conscious that they are regarded at all?

I set out one afternoon to go a-fishing to Fair-Haven, through the woods, to eke out my scanty fare of vegetables. My way led through Pleasant Meadow, an adjunct of the Baker Farm, that retreat of which a poet has since sung, beginning,—

“Thy entry is a pleasant field, Which some mossy fruit trees yield Partly to a ruddy brook, By gliding musquash undertook, And mercurial trout, Darting about.”

I thought of living there before I went to Walden. I “hooked” the apples, leaped the brook, and scared the musquash and the trout. It was one of those afternoons which seem indefinitely long before one, in which many events may happen, a large portion of our natural life, though it was already half spent when I started. By the way there came up a shower, which compelled me to stand half an hour under a pine, piling boughs over my head, and wearing my handkerchief for a shed; and when at length I had made one cast over the pickerel-weed, standing up to my middle in water, I found myself suddenly in the shadow of a cloud, and the thunder began to rumble with such emphasis that I could do no more than listen to it. The gods must be proud, thought I, with such forked flashes to rout a poor unarmed fisherman. So I made haste for shelter to the nearest hut, which stood half a mile from any road, but so much the nearer to the pond, and had long been uninhabited:—

“And here a poet builded, In the completed years, For behold a trivial cabin That to destruction steers.”

So the Muse fables. But therein, as I found, dwelt now John Field, an Irishman, and his wife, and several children, from the broad-faced boy who assisted his father at his work, and now came running by his side from the bog to escape the rain, to the wrinkled, sibyl-like, cone-headed infant that sat upon its father’s knee as in the palaces of nobles, and looked out from its home in the midst of wet and hunger inquisitively upon the stranger, with the privilege of infancy, not knowing but it was the last of a noble line, and the hope and cynosure of the world, instead of John Field’s poor starveling brat. There we sat together under that part of the roof which leaked the least, while it showered and thundered without. I had sat there many times of old before the ship was built that floated his family to America. An honest, hard-working, but shiftless man plainly was John Field; and his wife, she too was brave to cook so many successive dinners in the recesses of that lofty stove; with round greasy face and bare breast, still thinking to improve her condition one day; with the never absent mop in one hand, and yet no effects of it visible anywhere.

## 白话
有一次，我恰好站在彩虹拱桥的尽头，那彩虹充满了低层大气，染红了周围的草和树叶，使我仿佛透过彩色的水晶看世界，炫得我目眩神迷。那是一片彩虹的湖水，我在这片光中，短时间里像一只海豚般生活。如果这景象能持续更久，也许它会染上我的工作和生活。当我走在铁道桥上时，我常常惊叹于我影子周围那圈光晕，幻想着自己是被选中的人。有位来访我的人说，他以前见过一些爱尔兰人的影子，周围并没有光晕，只有本地人（即他）才如此特别。贝内文托·切利尼在他的回忆录中告诉我们，他在圣天使城堡被囚禁期间，曾做过一个可怕的梦或幻象，之后每天早晚，无论他在意大利还是法国，头上都会出现一道灿烂的光晕，尤其在草叶沾满露水时更为明显。这大概就是我刚才提到的同一现象，它尤其常见于清晨，但也可能在其他时候，甚至在月光下出现。虽然它是一种常现的现象，但很少被人注意，对于像切利尼这样易激动的想象力来说，这足以成为迷信的根据。此外，他还告诉我们，他只向极少数人展示过这光晕。但难道不是那些意识到自己被注意的人，才是真正特别的吗？

我有一天下午出发去福尔海文钓鱼，穿过树林，以补充我蔬菜微薄的伙食。我的路经过普莱斯利草地，这是贝克农场的一部分，一位诗人后来曾这样吟咏过：

“你进入的是一片愉快的田野，  
一些长满青苔的果树  
部分地让给一条红润的小溪，  
被滑溜的麝鼠所占据，  
还有敏捷的鳟鱼，  
在水中来回游动。”

在我去瓦尔登湖之前，我曾想过住在那儿。我“钓”到了苹果，跳过了小溪，吓跑了麝鼠和鳟鱼。那是我以前觉得漫长无尽的一个下午，许多事情可能发生，这占据了我们自然生活的一大块，而当我出发时，它已经过了一半。路上，下起了雨，我不得不在松树下站了半小时，用树枝遮住头顶，还用手帕当伞。最后，当我站在水里，腰深的水里，投下第一根鱼钩，突然间我发现自己处在一片云影之下，雷声轰鸣，声势如此强烈，我只能停下听它。我想，神明一定很骄傲，用如此分叉的闪电来驱赶一个无依无靠的渔夫。于是，我赶紧跑到最近的棚屋去避雨，那座棚屋离任何道路有半英里远，但离池塘更近，而且早已无人居住：

“这里曾有一位诗人建造，  
在岁月完成之时，  
看吧，一个平凡的小屋，  
正走向毁灭。”

所以诗人编造。但事实上，我后来发现，那里住着约翰·菲尔德，一个爱尔兰人，和他的妻子，还有几个孩子，从那个面庞宽厚的男孩开始，他帮助父亲干活，现在正从沼泽跑来，跟在父亲身边，逃避雨水；到那个皱纹纵横、像女先知般、头呈锥形的婴儿，坐在父亲膝上，仿佛在贵族的宫殿里，好奇地从家中，透过湿冷和饥饿，窥视着陌生人，带着婴儿的特权，不知道这是否是某个贵族世家的最后血脉，是否是世界的希望和焦点，而不是约翰·菲尔德家那个穷困的婴儿。我们坐在屋顶漏雨最少的地方，外面大雨倾盆，雷声隆隆。在我去瓦尔登湖之前，我曾多次坐在那里，那时他的家人还住在船上，漂浮在大海上，后来才来到美洲。约翰·菲尔德是个诚实、勤劳但懒散的人；他的妻子，也勇敢地在那高高的炉灶深处连续煮好几顿饭；她圆圆的脸上沾满油污，胸膛赤裸，仍想着有一天能改善自己的处境；她一只手始终拿着抹布，但你却看不到任何效果。
