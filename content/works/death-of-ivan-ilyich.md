## 原文
During an interval in the Melvinski trial in the large building of the Law Courts the members and public prosecutor met in Ivan Egorovich Shebek's private room, where the conversation turned on the celebrated Krasovski case. Fedor Vasilievich warmly maintained that it was not subject to their jurisdiction, Ivan Egorovich maintained the contrary, while Peter Ivanovich, not having entered into the discussion at the start, took no part in it but looked through the Gazette which had just been handed in. "Gentlemen," he said, "Ivan Ilych has died!" "You don't say so!" "Here, read it yourself," r Peter Ivanovich, handing Fedor Vasilievich the paper still damp from the press. Surrounded by a black border were the words: "Praskovya Fedorovna Golovina, with profound sorrow, informs relatives and friends of the demise of her beloved husband Ivan Ilych Golovin, Member of the Court of Justice, which occurred on February the 4th of this year 1882. the funeral will take place on Friday at one o'clock in the afternoon." Ivan Ilych had been a colleague of the gentlemen present and was liked by them all. He had been ill for some weeks with an illness said to be incurable. His post had been kept open for him, but there had been conjectures that in case of his death Alexeev might receive his appointment, and that either Vinnikov or Shtabel would succeed Alexeev. So on receiving the news of Ivan Ilych's death the first thought of each of the gentlemen in that private room was of the changes and promotions it might occasion among themselves or their acquaintances. "I shall be sure to get Shtabel's place or Vinnikov's," thought Fedor Vasilievich. "I was promised that long ago, and the promotion means an extra eight hundred rubles a year for me besides the allowance." "Now I must apply for my brother-in-law's transfer from Kaluga," thought Peter Ivanovich. "My wife will be very glad, and then she won't be able to say that I never do anything for her relations." "I thought he would never leave his bed again," said Peter Ivanovich aloud. "It's very sad." "But what really was the matter with him?" "The doctors couldn't say—at least they could, but each of them said something different. When last I saw him I thought he was getting better." "And I haven't been to see him since the holidays. I always meant to go." "Had he any property?" "I think his wife had a little—but something quite trifling." "We shall have to go to see her, but they live so terribly far away." "Far away from you, you mean. Everything's far away from your place." "You see, he never can forgive my living on the other side of the river," said Peter Ivanovich, smiling at Shebek. Then, still talking of the distances between different parts of the city, they returned to the Court. Besides considerations as to the possible transfers and promotions likely to result from Ivan Ilych's death, the mere fact of the death of a near acquaintance aroused, as usual, in all who heard of it the complacent feeling that, "it is he who is dead and not I." Each one thought or felt, "Well, he's dead but I'm alive!" But the more intimate of Ivan Ilych's acquaintances, his so-called friends, could not help thinking also that they would now have to fulfill the very tiresome demands of propriety by attending the funeral service and paying a visit of condolence to the widow. Fedor Vasilievich and Peter Ivanovich had been his nearest acquaintances. Peter Ivanovich had studied law with Ivan Ilych and had considered himself to be under obligations to him. Having told his wife at dinner-time of Ivan Ilych's death, and of his conjecture that it might be possible to get her brother transferred to their circuit, Peter Ivanovich sacrificed his usual nap, put on his evening clothes and drove to Ivan Ilych's house. At the entrance stood a carriage and two cabs. Leaning against the wall in the hall downstairs near the cloakstand was a coffin-lid covered with cloth of gold, ornamented with gold cord and tassels, that had been polished up with metal powder. Two ladies in black were taking off their fur cloaks. Peter Ivanovich recognized one of them as Ivan Ilych's sister, but the other was a stranger to him.

## 白话
在梅尔文斯基案休庭期间，法律大厦里的成员和公诉人聚集在伊凡·叶戈罗维奇·谢别克的私人房间内，谈话的内容是著名的克拉索夫斯基案。费多尔·瓦西里耶维奇热情地坚持认为这不在他们的管辖范围之内，而伊凡·叶戈罗维奇则持相反意见。彼得·伊万诺维奇一开始没有参与讨论，因此也没有加入谈话，只是翻阅着刚刚递来的报纸。“先生们，”他说，“伊凡·伊里奇去世了！”“你说什么？”“你自己看看吧，”彼得·伊万诺维奇说着，把那张还带着油墨味的报纸递给费多尔·瓦西里耶维奇。报纸四周被黑色边框包围，上面写着：“普拉斯科维娅·费多罗夫娜·戈洛维娜怀着深切的悲痛，向亲友通告她深爱的丈夫伊凡·伊里奇·戈洛文先生的逝世，这发生在今年二月四日，1882年。葬礼将于本周五下午一点举行。”伊凡·伊里奇是与在场各位同为同事的人，大家都喜欢他。他已病卧数周，据说病情无法治愈。他的职位一直保留着，但人们猜测如果他去世，阿列克谢耶夫可能会获得他的职位，而维尼科夫或斯塔贝尔可能会接替阿列克谢耶夫。因此，当得知伊凡·伊里奇去世的消息后，房间里的各位先生首先想到的，是这可能带来的变动和晋升机会。“我肯定能获得斯塔贝尔或维尼科夫的职位，”费多尔·瓦西里耶维奇心想，“我早就有这个希望，这次晋升还意味着我每年可以多拿八百卢布的额外收入。”“我得赶紧申请把我妹夫从卡卢加调过来，”彼得·伊万诺维奇心想，“我妻子一定会很高兴，她以后就再也说不出我从不为她亲戚做点什么这样的话了。”“我原以为他再也不会离开床了，”彼得·伊万诺维奇大声说道，“这太令人伤心了。”“他到底得了什么病？”“医生们说不清楚——至少他们可以说，但每个人的说法都不一样。上次我见到他时，我以为他正在好转。”“我从假期后就没去看他了，我本来一直打算去的。”“他有什么财产吗？”“我想他妻子有一些，不过只是些微不足道的东西。”“我们得去见见她，只是他们住得太远了。”“你是说离你们远，”“一切对你来说都远。”“你瞧，他就是不能原谅我住在河对岸，”彼得·伊万诺维奇笑着对谢别克说。接着，他们仍谈论着城市各处之间的距离，然后又回到了法院。除了考虑伊凡·伊里奇的死可能带来的职位调动和晋升外，听到熟人去世的消息，通常也会在每个人心中唤起一种自鸣得意的感觉：“是他死了，而不是我。”每个人心里都在想或感觉：“他死了，而我还活着！”然而，伊凡·伊里奇的亲密朋友，也就是所谓的他的朋友们，也不得不想到，他们现在必须履行礼仪上的种种繁琐义务，去参加葬礼并拜访寡妇。费多尔·瓦西里耶维奇和彼得·伊万诺维奇是他的近亲。彼得·伊万诺维奇曾与伊凡·伊里奇一起学习法律，认为自己对他负有某种义务。他在晚餐时告诉妻子伊凡·伊里奇去世的消息，并猜测或许可以将他妹夫调到他们的辖区，于是彼得·伊万诺维奇放弃了他平时的午睡，穿上晚礼服，开车前往伊凡·伊里奇的家中。门口停着一辆马车和两辆出租车。在楼下大厅的墙边，靠近大衣架的地方，放着一个用金丝织物覆盖的棺材盖，上面装饰着金线和流苏，还用金属粉擦得锃亮。两位穿黑衣的女士正在脱掉她们的皮大衣。彼得·伊万诺维奇认出其中一位是伊凡·伊里奇的妹妹，另一位他则不认识。
