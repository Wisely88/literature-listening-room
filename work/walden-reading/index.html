原文

2 段

The student may read Homer or Æschylus in the Greek without danger of dissipation or luxuriousness, for it implies that he in some measure emulate their heroes, and consecrate morning hours to their pages. The heroic books, even if printed in the character of our mother tongue, will always be in a language dead to degenerate times; and we must laboriously seek the meaning of each word and line, conjecturing a larger sense than common use permits out of what wisdom and valor and generosity we have. The modern cheap and fertile press, with all its translations, has done little to bring us nearer to the heroic writers of antiquity. They seem as solitary, and the letter in which they are printed as rare and curious, as ever. It is worth the expense of youthful days and costly hours, if you learn only some words of an ancient language, which are raised out of the trivialness of the street, to be perpetual suggestions and provocations. It is not in vain that the farmer remembers and repeats the few Latin words which he has heard. Men sometimes speak as if the study of the classics would at length make way for more modern and practical studies; but the adventurous student will always study classics, in whatever language they may be written and however ancient they may be. For what are the classics but the noblest recorded thoughts of man? They are the only oracles which are not decayed, and there are such answers to the most modern inquiry in them as Delphi and Dodona never gave. We might as well omit to study Nature because she is old. To read well, that is, to read true books in a true spirit, is a noble exercise, and one that will task the reader more than any exercise which the customs of the day esteem. It requires a training such as the athletes underwent, the steady intention almost of the whole life to this object. Books must be read as deliberately and reservedly as they were written. It is not enough even to be able to speak the language of that nation by which they are written, for there is a memorable interval between the spoken and the written language, the language heard and the language read. The one is commonly transitory, a sound, a tongue, a dialect merely, almost brutish, and we learn it unconsciously, like the brutes, of our mothers. The other is the maturity and experience of that; if that is our mother tongue, this is our father tongue, a reserved and select expression, too significant to be heard by the ear, which we must be born again in order to speak. The crowds of men who merely _spoke_ the Greek and Latin tongues in the middle ages were not entitled by the accident of birth to _read_ the works of genius written in those languages; for these were not written in that Greek or Latin which they knew, but in the select language of literature. They had not learned the nobler dialects of Greece and Rome, but the very materials on which they were written were waste paper to them, and they prized instead a cheap contemporary literature. But when the several nations of Europe had acquired distinct though rude written languages of their own, sufficient for the purposes of their rising literatures, then first learning revived, and scholars were enabled to discern from that remoteness the treasures of antiquity. What the Roman and Grecian multitude could not _hear_, after the lapse of ages a few scholars _read_, and a few scholars only are still reading it.

However much we may admire the orator’s occasional bursts of eloquence, the noblest written words are commonly as far behind or above the fleeting spoken language as the firmament with its stars is behind the clouds. _There_ are the stars, and they who can may read them. The astronomers forever comment on and observe them. They are not exhalations like our daily colloquies and vaporous breath. What is called eloquence in the forum is commonly found to be rhetoric in the study. The orator yields to the inspiration of a transient occasion, and speaks to the mob before him, to those who can _hear_ him; but the writer, whose more equable life is his occasion, and who would be distracted by the event and the crowd which inspire the orator, speaks to the intellect and health of mankind, to all in any age who can _understand_ him.

现代译文

学生若在希腊语中阅读荷马或埃斯奇勒斯，不会陷入放纵或奢侈之中，因为这暗示着他某种程度上要效仿这些英雄，将清晨的时光奉献给他们的文字。英雄的著作，即使以我们母语印刷，也始终是一种对堕落时代已死的语言；我们必须费力地探寻每个词和句子的含义，凭借我们所拥有的智慧、勇气与慷慨，推测出比日常用法更深远的意义。现代廉价而丰富的出版物，尽管提供了无数翻译，却并未使我们更接近古代的英雄作家。他们似乎依旧孤独，以它们印刷的字体而言，也依旧稀有而奇特，一如往昔。若你仅学会一些古代语言的词汇，这些词汇从街头的琐碎中被提升出来，成为永恒的提示与激励，那么这青春岁月的花费与宝贵时光的代价是值得的。农夫记住并重复他所听到的几个拉丁词汇，也并非徒劳。人们有时似乎认为，对经典的研习终将让位于更现代、更实用的学问；但勇敢的求知者将始终研习经典，无论它们是用何种语言写成，无论它们多么古老。因为经典不正是人类最崇高的思想记录吗？它们是唯一未衰败的预言，其中对最现代的疑问所给出的答案，远胜德尔斐与多多纳的神谕。我们不妨因为自然年迈而忽视研究自然。真正地阅读，也就是以真诚的精神阅读真正的书籍，是一种高尚的活动，它对读者的要求，比当今习俗所推崇的任何活动都更为严苛。这需要一种训练，如同运动员所经历的那样，几乎将整个生命都专注于此。书籍必须像它们被写成那样，被慎重而克制地阅读。即使你能说写书的那个民族的语言，也还不够，因为书面语与口语之间，存在一段值得铭记的距离，即听到的语言与阅读的语言之间。前者通常是短暂的，一种声音、一种语言、一种方言，几乎野蛮，我们是无意识地，像野兽一样，从母亲那里学会它的。后者则是前者的成熟与经验；如果那是我们的母语，那么这便是我们的父语，一种保留而精选的表达，意义深远，无法仅凭耳朵听到，我们必须重生才能言说。中世纪那些仅仅会说希腊语和拉丁语的人，没有因为出生的偶然而有权阅读用这些语言写成的天才作品；因为这些作品并非用他们所知的希腊语或拉丁语写成，而是用文学的精选语言写成的。他们没有学会希腊和罗马的高贵方言，而他们所使用的书写材料对他们而言不过是废纸，他们反而更看重廉价的当代文学。但当欧洲各国获得了自己独特而粗陋的书面语言，足以满足他们正在兴起的文学需求时，学习才重新兴起，学者们才得以从那遥远的过去中，辨识出古代的宝藏。罗马与希腊的大众在岁月流逝后无法听到的东西，少数学者得以阅读，而如今，也只有少数学者仍在阅读它。

尽管我们可能钦佩演说家偶尔的雄辩，但最崇高的书面语言通常与转瞬即逝的口语相距甚远，就像星空与云层之间相距甚远。星空在那里，能读到它们的人便可阅读。天文学家们永远在评论与观察它们。它们并非如我们日常对话和雾气般的言语。论坛中所谓的雄辩，通常在书房中被发现为修辞。演说家屈从于短暂场合的灵感，对面前的群众讲话，对那些能听到他的人讲话；而作者，其更平稳的生活便是他的场合，他若被演说家所受的事件与人群所干扰，便会对全人类的理智与健康讲话，对任何时代中能理解他的人讲话。

作者

亨利·戴维·梭罗

1817—1862 · 近代 · 美国

亨利·戴维·梭罗（Henry David Thoreau，1817—1862），美国作家、思想家与自然观察者，超验主义的重要代表。《瓦尔登湖》记录了他在康科德瓦尔登湖畔简朴生活期间对自然、劳动与自我独立的思考。

文学风格

文字清澈、节制而富有哲思，常从细小的自然经验抵达关于生活方式与精神自由的追问。
