## 原文
The Time Traveller (for so it will be convenient to speak of him) was expounding a recondite matter to us. His grey eyes shone and twinkled, and his usually pale face was flushed and animated. The fire burnt brightly, and the soft radiance of the incandescent lights in the lilies of silver caught the bubbles that flashed and passed in our glasses. Our chairs, being his patents, embraced and caressed us rather than submitted to be sat upon, and there was that luxurious after-dinner atmosphere, when thought runs gracefully free of the trammels of precision. And he put it to us in this way—marking the points with a lean forefinger—as we sat and lazily admired his earnestness over this new paradox (as we thought it) and his fecundity.

“You must follow me carefully. I shall have to controvert one or two ideas that are almost universally accepted. The geometry, for instance, they taught you at school is founded on a misconception.”

“Is not that rather a large thing to expect us to begin upon?” said Filby, an argumentative person with red hair.

“I do not mean to ask you to accept anything without reasonable ground for it. You will soon admit as much as I need from you. You know of course that a mathematical line, a line of thickness _nil_, has no real existence. They taught you that? Neither has a mathematical plane. These things are mere abstractions.”

“That is all right,” said the Psychologist.

“Nor, having only length, breadth, and thickness, can a cube have a real existence.”

“There I object,” said Filby. “Of course a solid body may exist. All real things—”

“So most people think. But wait a moment. Can an _instantaneous_ cube exist?”

“Don’t follow you,” said Filby.

“Can a cube that does not last for any time at all, have a real existence?”

Filby became pensive. “Clearly,” the Time Traveller proceeded, “any real body must have extension in _four_ directions: it must have Length, Breadth, Thickness, and—Duration. But through a natural infirmity of the flesh, which I will explain to you in a moment, we incline to overlook this fact. There are really four dimensions, three which we call the three planes of Space, and a fourth, Time. There is, however, a tendency to draw an unreal distinction between the former three dimensions and the latter, because it happens that our consciousness moves intermittently in one direction along the latter from the beginning to the end of our lives.”

“That,” said a very young man, making spasmodic efforts to relight his cigar over the lamp; “that . . . very clear indeed.”

“Now, it is very remarkable that this is so extensively overlooked,” continued the Time Traveller, with a slight accession of cheerfulness. “Really this is what is meant by the Fourth Dimension, though some people who talk about the Fourth Dimension do not know they mean it. It is only another way of looking at Time. _There is no difference between Time and any of the three dimensions of Space except that our consciousness moves along it_. But some foolish people have got hold of the wrong side of that idea. You have all heard what they have to say about this Fourth Dimension?”

“_I_ have not,” said the Provincial Mayor.

“It is simply this. That Space, as our mathematicians have it, is spoken of as having three dimensions, which one may call Length, Breadth, and Thickness, and is always definable by reference to three planes, each at right angles to the others. But some philosophical people have been asking why _three_ dimensions particularly—why not another direction at right angles to the other three?—and have even tried to construct a Four-Dimensional geometry. Professor Simon Newcomb was expounding this to the New York Mathematical Society only a month or so ago. You know how on a flat surface, which has only two dimensions, we can represent a figure of a three-dimensional solid, and similarly they think that by models of three dimensions they could represent one of four—if they could master the perspective of the thing. See?”

“I think so,” murmured the Provincial Mayor; and, knitting his brows, he lapsed into an introspective state, his lips moving as one who repeats mystic words. “Yes, I think I see it now,” he said after some time, brightening in a quite transitory manner.

## 白话
时间旅行者（这样说比较方便）正在向我们阐述一个深奥的问题。他的灰眼睛闪闪发亮，脸色通常苍白，此刻却泛红而神采飞扬。壁炉燃烧得正旺，银莲花灯发出柔和的光，映照着我们酒杯中闪烁而过的气泡。我们的椅子是他的专利设计，它们环抱着我们，仿佛在亲昵地抚慰，而不是仅仅供人坐的。空气中弥漫着那种舒适的饭后氛围，思想在精确的束缚之外自由地流淌。他这样向我们解释——用他那细长的手指指着重点，而我们坐着，懒洋洋地欣赏着他对于这个新奇悖论（我们认为是的）的认真态度，以及他丰富的想象力。

“你们必须仔细听我。我必须反驳一些几乎被普遍接受的观点。比如说，你们在学校里学的几何学，是建立在一个误解之上的。”

“这不算是一个很轻松的任务吧？”菲利比说，他是个爱争论的人，红头发。

“我不是要你们无根据地接受任何观点。你们很快就会承认我所需要的一切。你们当然知道，数学中的线，一条厚度为零的线，是没有真实存在的。他们教过你们吗？同样，数学中的平面也没有真实存在。这些都是纯粹的抽象概念。”

“这倒是没错，”心理学家说道。

“而且，既然只有长、宽、厚三个维度，一个立方体也不可能有真实的存在。”

“我反对，”菲利比说，“当然，一个实体可以存在。所有真实的事物——”

“大多数人都是这么想的。但请等一下。一个‘瞬间’存在的立方体，能有真实的存在吗？”

“我不太明白你的意思，”菲利比说。

“一个不持续任何时间的立方体，能有真实的存在吗？”

菲利比陷入沉思。“很明显，”时间旅行者继续说道，“任何真实存在的物体，都必须在四个方向上具有延伸：它必须有长、宽、厚，还有——时间。但因为我们身体的自然缺陷，我稍后会向你们解释，我们习惯于忽略这一点。实际上，存在四个维度，其中三个是我们称为空间的三个平面，还有一个是时间。不过，人们倾向于不恰当地把前三个维度和这个维度区分开来，因为我们的意识在生命开始到结束的过程中，只是沿着时间这个维度间断地移动。”

“啊，”一个年轻人说道，他正努力在灯下重新点着他的雪茄；“啊……这……非常清楚。”

“现在，这被如此广泛地忽视，实在很值得注意，”时间旅行者带着一丝轻松的语气继续说道。“实际上，这就是所谓的第四维度，尽管有些人谈论第四维度时并不知道自己说的是什么。它只是另一种看待时间的方式。_时间与空间的三个维度之间唯一的区别，就是我们的意识沿着时间移动_。但有些愚蠢的人误解了这个想法。你们都听过他们对第四维度的说法吗？”

“_我没听过，”省长说。

“这很简单。空间，正如我们的数学家所定义的，被认为有三个维度，我们可以称之为长、宽、厚，而且总是可以通过三个互相垂直的平面来定义。但有些哲学家一直在问，为什么偏偏是三个维度？为什么不能有另一个方向，与另外三个垂直？他们甚至尝试构建一种四维几何学。西蒙·纽科姆教授上个月刚在纽约数学学会上阐述过这个观点。你们知道，在一个只有二维的平面上，我们能够用二维的图形来表现三维的立体，他们认为，如果他们掌握了四维物体的透视法，就可以用三维的模型来表现四维的物体。明白吗？”

“我想我明白了，”省长低声说道，皱着眉头陷入沉思，嘴唇翕动，仿佛在重复神秘的词句。“过了一会儿，他说，‘我想我现在明白了。’他脸上闪过一丝短暂的光彩。”
